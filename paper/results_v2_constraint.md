# Results V2 — hard NDC physical-consistency formulation

> **Status:** diagnostic scientific draft. This version implements the intended interpretation of Engine 3 as a hard physical-consistency constraint. It is not yet the final manuscript result because the audit exposed a second issue: some transition CAPEX is currently allocated directly to operating sectors that are themselves physically constrained (notably transmission, refining, and biofuels). That mapping should be cleaned before the paper results are frozen.

## 3.1 Two transmission channels followed by an NDC consistency condition

The preferred model interpretation contains two economic transmission channels. First, transition investment changes final demand for domestic capital goods, construction, infrastructure, and related services. Second, the NDC energy pathway changes intermediate input requirements through a scenario-specific technical-coefficient matrix, shifting demand between fossil fuels, bioenergy, and electricity.

Selected physical production trajectories from the NDC pathway then enter as output constraints rather than as an additional autonomous demand shock. Let C denote the constrained sectors and let

\[
L_t^*=(I-A_t^*)^{-1}
\]

be the Leontief inverse after energy-input rewiring. The unconstrained transition solution is

\[
\Delta x_t^{U}=L_t^*\left(\Delta f_{GDP,t}+\Delta f_{INV,t}\right).
\]

For constrained sectors, the NDC pathway provides the target output change \(\Delta x_{C,t}^{NDC}\). A residual final-demand vector \(g_{C,t}\) is solved such that

\[
g_{C,t}=(L_{CC,t}^*)^{-1}\left(\Delta x_{C,t}^{NDC}-\Delta x_{C,t}^{U}\right),
\]

and

\[
\Delta x_t^{NDC}=\Delta x_t^{U}+L_{:,C,t}^*g_{C,t}.
\]

This formulation guarantees that the selected sectoral outputs exactly match the NDC physical trajectory while preserving the production-network response generated by the rewired input structure. Numerical constraint error is zero to machine precision in the audit.

## 3.2 What happens before the physical constraints are imposed?

The unconstrained production network is strongly expansionary because transition investment activates domestic construction, machinery, metals, engineering, and service supply chains. In 2050, energy-input rewiring alone adds approximately R$16.1 billion of value added relative to the GDP-only baseline. Transition investment, evaluated after rewiring, adds a further R$488.9 billion.

Thus, before the physical production constraints are imposed, the modeled transition generates roughly R$505 billion of additional value added relative to the no-transition GDP-growth counterfactual.

This is the economically endogenous part of the model: the production network responds to the changed composition of intermediate energy demand and to the additional transition-investment vector.

## 3.3 How much adjustment is required to satisfy the NDC production pathway?

Imposing the physical NDC trajectories requires a substantial residual adjustment in the constrained sectors. In the current parameterization, the 2050 NDC consistency adjustment reduces value added by approximately R$560.1 billion relative to the unconstrained transition solution. The resulting hard-constrained value-added differential is therefore approximately **−R$55.1 billion** in 2050 relative to the GDP-only counterfactual.

The result is strongly time-dependent. The hard-constrained value-added differential is approximately +R$559 billion in 2030, +R$411 billion in 2035, and +R$292 billion in 2040. It becomes slightly negative by 2045 (−R$64.5 billion) and remains close to zero but negative in 2050 (−R$55.1 billion).

This is a materially different result from the earlier additive Engine-3 architecture. It implies that the transition investment wave generates a large positive production-network impulse in the 2030s, but the NDC production constraints become increasingly binding as the physical trajectories diverge from GDP-driven growth.

These values should be treated as diagnostic rather than final because the hard-constraint audit revealed that some investment expenditure is currently mapped directly to sectors whose operating output is later constrained by the physical pathway. In such cases, part of the consistency adjustment is mechanically undoing the CAPEX shock rather than only reconciling energy-system production.

## 3.4 Oil: how much decline already comes from energy rewiring?

Oil and gas extraction provides a useful illustration of the distinction between endogenous demand effects and the NDC production constraint.

In 2050, a GDP-only expansion would imply an output increase of approximately R$284.3 billion relative to the 2018 base. Energy-input rewiring lowers that output path by approximately R$102.5 billion before any physical oil constraint is imposed. This is the endogenous production-network response to lower petroleum requirements in downstream activities.

Transition investment adds only about R$6.7 billion to oil and gas output indirectly through the broader supplier network, leaving the unconstrained transition solution at approximately +R$188.5 billion relative to the base.

The NDC physical trajectory, however, requires oil and gas output to be approximately R$130.7 billion below the base-year level. The remaining gap is therefore about **−R$319.2 billion** of output. Under the hard-constraint formulation, this gap is closed by a residual final-demand/export balancing term of approximately **−R$275.6 billion** in the oil and gas sector, with the remainder transmitted through inter-industry linkages.

The interpretation is important: the decline in oil production is not attributed solely to electrification or other domestic demand changes. Part of the decline emerges endogenously from lower intermediate demand, while the remainder reflects the externally specified production/export trajectory embedded in the NDC scenario.

## 3.5 The hard-constraint audit exposes a CAPEX-mapping issue

The audit also identifies a structural issue that should be corrected before publication. Several constrained sectors receive transition investment directly in the current investment-allocation routine. For example, transmission investment is partly allocated to the transmission operating sector; biofuel investment is partly allocated directly to biofuel production; and refinery upgrades are partly allocated to refining.

When those same sectors are subsequently constrained to their NDC physical output trajectories, the hard constraint can cancel a large share of the investment-driven output increase. This is visible in 2050: the unconstrained output of transmission, biofuels, refining, and electricity-related activities can exceed the physical target substantially before the consistency condition is imposed.

Conceptually, transition CAPEX should primarily enter the sectors that **produce the capital goods and construction services** required by the energy system, rather than automatically increasing the contemporaneous operating output of the sector receiving the asset. The next model revision should therefore separate:

- investment recipients/users of capital, such as electricity generation, transmission, refining, and biofuel production; from
- suppliers of investment goods and services, such as construction, machinery, electrical equipment, metals, engineering, and specialized services.

This distinction is likely to reduce the artificial conflict between the investment channel and the physical-output constraints and will provide a cleaner estimate of the transition premium.

## 3.6 Implication for the paper

The hard-constraint formulation changes the central scientific question in a useful way. Rather than asking only whether low-carbon investment offsets fossil contraction, the model can identify three sequential quantities:

1. the effect of changing intermediate energy requirements;
2. the additional production-network impulse from transition investment; and
3. the residual adjustment required to make the resulting economy consistent with the NDC physical production pathway.

The oil example can then distinguish how much sectoral change arises endogenously from domestic demand restructuring and how much must be imposed to satisfy the external production trajectory.

## Current paper takeaway

> **The NDC transition generates a strong domestic investment impulse, but the final macroeconomic outcome depends on how far the endogenous production-network response lies from the physical production pathway. A hard consistency formulation reveals that part of the fossil decline already emerges from changing intermediate demand, while the remaining adjustment reflects the externally specified NDC production trajectory.**

Before freezing this result, the investment-allocation matrix should be revised so that CAPEX is represented as demand for capital-goods and construction suppliers rather than as contemporaneous operating output in physically constrained energy sectors.
